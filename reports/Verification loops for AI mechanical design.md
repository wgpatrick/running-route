# Open verifier loops stop short of motion

For a Claude-plus-build123d agent built as CAD → verifiers → more CAD, the open state of the art in November 2026 covers the static half of the problem and leaves the moving half to you. One MIT-licensed project, earthtojake/text-to-cad (26.2k stars, version 0.11.9), already ships exact all-pairs assembly interference at named poses, joint-stop sweeps, bonded-assembly linear-static FEA on CalculiX, per-process manufacturability checks, and MJCF/URDF export from build123d joints with a MuJoCo compile-and-health check. All of that verifier code landed in the week of 7–12 November 2026, has no published accuracy evidence, and was read but not run for this report. No harness whose code was read runs a dynamic simulation as a pass/fail verifier, solves closed kinematic loops, checks whether parts can be assembled or reached with a tool, runs contact or bolted-joint FEA, or does tolerance analysis; PartCAD's README claims some of these but its implementation was not inspected. The benchmarks say the verifiers matter more than the generator: the best model on the MUSE assembly benchmark executes 99% of designs but only 58% pass joint checks, and frontier agents almost never satisfy strict FEA requirements on a first attempt. Off-the-shelf parts are constrained by licences more than by technology: the only clean route is parametric generation (bd_warehouse, Apache-2.0) plus a small in-house typed catalogue, because the large vendor catalogues restrict bulk, automated or AI use. For a small team the build order that follows is: pin and trial text-to-cad in your sandbox, then build an assembly linter, a kinematics-first motion verifier with MuJoCo inverse dynamics, modal and tool-point stiffness FEA, a cheap directional assemblability check, and a typed parts catalogue with sizing checkers, in that order.

**How to read the evidence.** The researchers could read github.com directly but mostly could not open arxiv.org, huggingface.co, vendor sites or documentation hosts. This report keeps three labels. *First-hand* means the repository file or page was read in this session. *Search summary* means the claim comes from a search-result summary of a page that could not be opened; treat numbers as unverified until checked against the source. *Inference* means a design judgement by the researchers or by this report, not a sourced fact. One researcher did reach arXiv abstracts and HTML, so the assembly-benchmark figures (MUSE, AssemCAD, ArtiCAD, CADEngBench assembly track, RealCADBench, Done Is Not Correct) are first-hand, while the FEA-loop papers and all motion and assembly-planning papers are search summaries. Model names such as "GPT-6 Astra" or "Claude Opus 4.7" are reported exactly as the papers give them and were not cross-checked. No tool described here was installed or run.

## Executable assemblies fail measured checks, so the loop must measure before it claims

Three results set the stakes. On MUSE, a multi-component B-rep benchmark, the strongest model executes 99.06% of designs and reaches 89.62% geometric validity but only 57.55% joint success, and Claude Opus 4.7 scores 76.42% execution with 23.58% joint success; the paper also finds that "correctly typed interfaces may still fit poorly" and that most models generate fewer holes than the reference ([MUSE, arXiv 2605.28579](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28579), first-hand). On AssemBench (120 small assemblies averaging 3.38 bodies), raw frontier-model code preserved assembly structure in 0.83% to 66.67% of cases against 87.50% for a typed-port system, though that metric is scored by an LLM judge and not by geometry ([AssemCAD, arXiv 2607.05123](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05123), first-hand). On the FEA side, the Hephaestus-CCX paper calls its benchmark "nearly unsolved under strict FEA grading": a secondary summary reports zero strict passes in 400 first attempts, while the paper's own table shows 1 of 30 multi-part passes for one configuration, so the exact count is unsettled ([arXiv 2605.17448](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17448), search summary; the earlier landscape report in this project recorded the zero-in-400 figure as checked against the primary page).

The most useful single finding for loop design is that the verifier's value depends on the harness forcing its use. In "Done Is Not Correct" (13 single-part tasks, 122 kernel-measured checks), single-shot agents claimed completion on 98% of attempts and were silently wrong on 14.1%; a loop that enforced measure-then-claim cut silent failures to **3.2%** and raised claim precision from 64% to 96%, and an ablation attributed the gain "to the claim contract rather than the feedback" ([arXiv 2611.10063](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10063), first-hand). The study has no assembly tier, so extending the result to assemblies is an inference. A practitioner test reached the same place from the other side: a CadQuery `union()` silently dropped a part's core while the solid still read as valid, and nine of 16 failures went unreported by the tool ([torno issue #42](https://github.com/luisg0nc/torno/issues/42), first-hand read of a third-party note).

Harness evidence is positive but modest, and all of it is self-reported. build123d-mcp states that using it raised one model's CADGenBench score from 0.360 to 0.457 and validity from 88% to 100% ([build123d-mcp README](https://github.com/pzfreo/build123d-mcp/blob/main/README.md), first-hand). agentcad reports 0.184 to 0.245 overall and 67/81 to 80/81 valid across two iterations of its harness on a small model, from single runs with no variance ([agentcad performance evidence](https://github.com/jdilla1277/agentcad/blob/main/docs/product-plans/agentcad-performance-evidence.md), first-hand). The gains concentrate in validity; absolute correctness scores of 0.25 to 0.46 show that geometry from drawings is unsolved even with a loop. No published benchmark with public code covers assemblies, mechanisms or FEA requirements together, and none tests agents on assemblies of 50 to 500 parts.

## Whole-assembly geometry checks have a known recipe and four open holes

### The interference recipe is settled: box filter, mesh "apart" filter, exact Boolean volume

Independent tools converged on the same pipeline in 2026. A bounding-box broad phase prunes pairs, an optional mesh test rules out pairs with air between them, and the verdict comes from the exact B-rep common volume, with a noise floor of about 0.001 mm³ and an explicit allow-list for intended overlaps. text-to-cad's `cad model interference` implements this across up to four processes, caches each pair by the two geometries plus their relative placement, and uses exit code 3 for "a pair could not be measured, which is no pass" ([text-to-cad PR #823](https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/pull/823), first-hand). Its reported cost on a humanoid model is about **10 minutes cold** for 1,802 box-overlapping pairs on four cores, 0.16 s on a repeat run from cache, and 16 s after one part edit; eight pairs of lofted panels took two to three minutes each. The inference is that per-pair caching keyed on geometry hash and relative transform is the single most important performance feature for an edit loop.

The move to exact volume was forced by a failure worth remembering. The project's own post-mortem records rails cutting through a shin guard "in a model I had reported as checked three times over", because a home-made check sampled mesh points and "a plate passing through a skin scored nothing"; the exact re-check found overlaps of 190, 10 and 2,677 mm³ ([galatea FEEDBACK.md](https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/blob/main/models/galatea/FEEDBACK.md), first-hand). That incident produced both the command and the skill's rule that "a check is trusted once it has been seen to fail".

A separate prototype measured where the OpenCASCADE primitives break ([laminarityai/cad issue #92](https://github.com/laminarityai/cad/issues/92), first-hand). Common-volume Booleans were correct on valid solids and took 1 to 50 ms on analytic parts and around a second on a 2019-face gear. Exact minimum distance (`BRepExtrema_DistShapeShape`) scaled roughly quadratically with face count: 209 ms for a 204-face plate, 35.7 s for a 3,084-face plate, **166 s** for two stacked spur gears. The pitfalls matter more than the timings. A part fully nested inside another reported a distance of 2.0 mm, not zero, so containment has to come from the overlap test. An invalid solid made the Boolean return 177,035 where the truth was 0. A non-zero fuzzy tolerance hid small overlaps, and multithreading made distance queries up to 3.6 times slower. The prototype's order of operations is the one to copy: validate first, compute overlap with fuzzy value zero, compute distance only when there is no overlap, and return `unknown` on any precondition failure, timeout or out-of-memory.

build123d's built-in `Compound.do_children_intersect` is a building block and not a verifier. It returns only the first offending pair, has no allow-list, does not distinguish containment, and uses a 1e-5 volume tolerance ([build123d composite.py](https://github.com/gumyr/build123d/blob/dev/src/build123d/topology/composite.py), first-hand). build123d-mcp's pairwise `clearance()` adds the four-way status that an agent can act on: `apart`, `touching`, `containing`, `interpenetrating` ([build123d-mcp llms.md](https://github.com/pzfreo/build123d-mcp/blob/main/llms.md), first-hand).

### Assembly representation favours closed-form mates over solvers

build123d stores an assembly as a labelled tree of shapes, and its joints "only [do] a one time re-position of a part and [do] not bind them in any way"; labels need not be unique, and `assembly.volume` returns only the top-level value, which is a trap for mass roll-up ([build123d joints.rst](https://github.com/gumyr/build123d/blob/dev/docs/joints.rst), [assemblies.rst](https://github.com/gumyr/build123d/blob/dev/docs/assemblies.rst), first-hand). CadQuery solves constraints numerically with CasADi and IPOPT, and its documentation warns that an under-constrained system leaves parts unmoved and that the starting position influences the solution ([cadquery assy.rst](https://github.com/CadQuery/cadquery/blob/master/doc/assy.rst), first-hand). FreeCAD's Assembly workbench has an open issue that "the solver is not deterministic when multiple solutions exist" ([FreeCAD issue #33557](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/issues/33557), first-hand), and a headless scripting attempt on 1.0.2 could create joints but not drive them ([constraint-kit issue #28](https://github.com/jajmangold/constraint-kit/issues/28), first-hand). Onshape has no REST endpoint for interference, per forum answers that are about four years old (search summary).

The inference is that deterministic, closed-form placement (build123d joints, AssemCAD's typed ports) suits a generate-verify-revise loop better than an iterative solver, because results are reproducible and a failure is attributable to one mate. The cost is no closed loops and no degree-of-freedom analysis. Because joints do not persist as constraints, the verifier has to re-derive "is this mate still satisfied" from final geometry, and your own code has to enforce unique part names before any report names a pair.

### Fits, fasteners, floating parts and tolerance are where the open tooling ends

The measurement primitives exist in build123d (`touch`, `distance_to_with_closest_points`, mass and inertia), and bd_warehouse fasteners carry clearance-hole diameters by fit class (Close, Normal, Loose), tap-drill tables by material class, and a `hole_locations` list recorded when holes are cut ([bd_warehouse fastener.py](https://github.com/gumyr/bd_warehouse/blob/main/src/bd_warehouse/fastener.py), first-hand). Four things were not found in any open library: a checker for fastener-to-hole compatibility and thread engagement, a coaxiality check for mating hole patterns across two parts, a "no floating parts" connectivity check, and an ISO 286 fit-class checker. The values of the underlying standards were not fetched either, so any fit or clearance table needs a separate standards lookup.

The pattern to copy for these comes from research systems. AssemCAD verifies each declared port against the B-rep by point classification: a bore must be empty along its axis and solid at a ring around it, a shaft the reverse. It maps recurring LLM failures to gates, the clearest being the "phantom bore", where a sign error makes a cut miss the solid so an axle passes through an undrilled arm ([AssemCAD, arXiv 2607.05123](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05123), first-hand). MUSE recovers a physical assembly graph from the STEP itself, with solids as nodes and typed interfaces as edges ([MUSE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.28579), first-hand). CADGenBench scores "would it bolt up to the same fixture?" with authored keep-in and keep-out sub-volumes, and one misplaced mating group drives that score to 0.00 while shape similarity stays at 0.89 ([cadgenbench metrics](https://github.com/huggingface/cadgenbench/blob/main/docs/metrics.md), first-hand). The inference for your loop is to check fasteners against declared metadata and not reverse-engineered geometry, to build a contact graph from the touching/overlapping classification and flag any part not reachable from a grounded part, and to use keep-in/keep-out volumes for envelopes and interfaces.

Tolerance analysis is the widest gap. Open tooling is limited to small 1D stack-up libraries such as dimstack (worst-case, RSS and six-sigma shown in its examples; Monte Carlo not confirmed) ([dimstack](https://github.com/phcreery/dimstack), first-hand). Nothing open derives the dimension chain from 3D geometry, AssemCAD explicitly scopes tolerance out, and a targeted search found no 2024–2026 paper on LLM tolerance allocation. Two inferences give a practical route: have the agent declare each critical gap as an explicit 1D loop tied to the script's parameters, and, because the model is parametric code, rebuild the assembly at tolerance extremes and re-run the interference and clearance checks as a brute-force substitute for 3D variation analysis.

## Motion verification is mostly kinematics; MuJoCo earns its place for loops and motor sizing

### text-to-cad already exports the model but stops before simulating it

The conversion problem is largely solved for tree mechanisms. text-to-cad takes build123d's `RevoluteJoint`, `LinearJoint`, `CylindricalJoint` and `BallJoint` as the source of truth and writes MJCF or URDF with meshes in metres; masses, centres of mass and inertia tensors come from the kernel and declared materials, and "the file is regenerated, never edited" ([robot-simulation SKILL.md](https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/blob/main/skills/robot-simulation/SKILL.md), first-hand). Gear pairs, rack-and-pinion and lead screws (`screw_pair`, pitch in mm per turn) export as MJCF joint equalities. `cad model ranges` sweeps each joint alone until the child link meets its parent, with a 0.3 mm default clearance. `cad mujoco check` compiles the model and applies rest-state health rules with stable codes, including `contacts_at_rest`, `scale_suspect` ("millimetres read as metres"), `inertia_from_visuals` and `mass_summary` ([mjcf-checks.md](https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/blob/main/skills/robot-simulation/references/mjcf-checks.md), first-hand).

Its limits are stated in its own words. The skill covers descriptions, "nor a simulation run"; the human opens MuJoCo Studio. On motion: "A pose or a motion shows movement; it does not measure it", so "a part that clears there can still pass through another mid-motion". On closed loops: "a loop's pairs are not posed (there is no solver)", so four-bars and slider-cranks cannot be posed, sequenced or range-swept, and "the closing joint's range is not enforced" ([kinematics.md](https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/blob/main/skills/cad/references/kinematics.md), first-hand). The interference tool also "takes the poses it is handed and chooses none itself". The exporter source was not inspected, so its behaviour on a full printer-scale assembly is unknown. No other standalone build123d-to-MJCF package was identified; the other converters are tied to Onshape, Fusion or SolidWorks.

### MuJoCo 4.0.1 is the pragmatic engine, with soft loop closure as the caveat

MuJoCo reached 4.0.0 on 6 November 2026 and 4.0.1 on 11 November; the 4.0.1 release fixes a sparse-Cholesky regression that caused "slow convergence, wrong accelerations and divergence", so constraint-heavy models should pin 4.0.1 or later ([MuJoCo changelog](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco/blob/main/doc/changelog.rst), first-hand). The `mujoco-core` wheel depends only on numpy and is described as "ideal for headless servers". The features a machine verifier needs are present: `connect` and `weld` equalities for loop closure, joint-to-joint polynomial coupling for gears and screws, tendon-to-tendon equality for belts, a `dcmotor` actuator, and the `MjSpec` programmatic model editor ([XMLreference.rst](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco/blob/main/doc/XMLreference.rst), first-hand). Two cautions apply. MuJoCo's loop closures are soft constraints enforced by the solver (search summary), so constraint residual must itself be a monitored pass/fail quantity. And MuJoCo shipped 15 releases in 2026 with breaking changes at 4.0, so generate models through `MjSpec` and pin the version.

The alternatives were assessed less firmly. Project Chrono 10.0.0 has dedicated gear, pulley and shaft elements but is distributed through conda only (search summary). Newton 1.8.0 (12 November 2026, Apache-2.0) adds loop-closing joints, routed tendons with capstan friction and a deterministic mode scoped to the same GPU, driver and build, but most of that is marked experimental ([Newton CHANGELOG](https://github.com/newton-physics/newton/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md), first-hand). Licences, install routes and current capabilities for Drake, PyBullet, Exudyn's belt elements, MBDyn and OpenModelica rest on the researcher's background knowledge and were not re-verified. No independent mechanism-focused benchmark comparing engines was found. The inference is that a single-machine verifier simulating one mechanism for a few seconds gains nothing from GPU engines.

### Split the checks: geometry for clearance, the engine for loops and forces

No authoritative checklist for motion verification of a machine design was found; what follows is the researchers' synthesis. Physics-engine contact is a poor primary clash detector for CAD, because meshes collide as convex hulls, small penetrations are tolerated by design, and nominally sliding pairs such as a carriage on its rail must be excluded anyway. Exact B-rep interference at sampled poses is more trustworthy. For a three-axis gantry the joint space is only three-dimensional, so a grid or Latin-hypercube sample over all joints together, not one at a time, is affordable given the pair cache. The engine's distinctive contributions are producing consistent poses for closed loops, which the CAD layer cannot, and producing forces.

For actuator sizing, inverse dynamics on a prescribed trajectory is the cheaper and more robust design: prescribe a trapezoid or S-curve at target acceleration, call inverse dynamics to obtain joint forces, reflect them through pulley radius or screw lead, and compare with the motor's torque-speed curve. This needs no contact and no controller tuning, and it is deterministic. The remaining checks in the proposed set are a per-joint nudge test that asserts a named frame moves in the intended direction, a numerical mobility test (the Grübler count is known to be wrong for the redundantly constrained geometries gantries use, per a search summary of [Wampler et al.](https://bertini.nd.edu:443/LocalDimFinder/Mechanism_Mobility.pdf)), a workspace-versus-build-volume check, gravity hold for the Z axis, and tipping stability. A rigid-body pass says nothing about resonance or input-shaper frequency; that belongs to modal FEA or a lumped model with belt stiffness.

### No open gantry model exists, so the printer case is first-principles work

Two searches found no published MuJoCo, Chrono or Gazebo model of a Voron, Prusa or CoreXY printer (search summary; hobby attempts on GitHub may exist). Academic CoreXY models are hand-derived and include belt stiffness and friction. The researchers propose an abstraction that was not found in any source: model the frame, gantry beam, carriage and bed as bodies on slide joints, do not simulate belts as bodies, and encode each CoreXY belt as a fixed tendon whose length constraint ties the two axis positions to a motor angle; represent lead screws as joint equalities, rails as slide joints with friction loss and travel range, and motors with rotor inertia as armature. Dual parallel rails are over-constrained, so one rail becomes the joint and the second a soft constraint whose force is a proxy for racking load. text-to-cad's documentation notes that MuJoCo cannot close a loop with a slide equality, which shapes how that closure must be written. For realistic trajectories, `klipper_estimator` has a `dump-moves` mode that writes planning data for every move ([klipper_estimator README](https://github.com/Annex-Engineering/klipper_estimator), first-hand), though its output format and units were not checked. All of this is untested design.

For showing motion to a person, MuJoCo 4.0 ships an official browser viewer (`mujoco-studio --gfx=web`) and a headless render sample that records multi-frame video ([MuJoCo changelog](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco/blob/main/doc/changelog.rst), first-hand), and text-to-cad plays joint sequences in its own CAD viewer without physics. Whether MuJoCo's newer Filament renderer works in a container without a GPU is unverified; classic offscreen OpenGL is the conservative choice.

Evidence on agents writing simulation models is thin for machines. The literature is dominated by articulated-object reconstruction, where a headline 75% success for Articulate-Anything falls to 40–49% under an independent re-evaluation (both search summaries). No benchmark measures an agent producing a simulation-ready gantry or gearbox from CAD. The transferable lesson is structural: derive the simulation model mechanically from CAD so that inertia, scale and frame errors cannot occur, and leave the model to author only the small reviewable residue of collision choices, friction, actuators and the test trajectory with its thresholds.

## Assemblability can be checked cheaply by reversing disassembly, with honest "no plan found" verdicts

### Disassembly planners exist as open code and solve about five in six

Every finding in this section other than the two GitHub repositories is a search summary. The most reusable open code is the MIT/Autodesk line. Assemble-Them-All and ASAP are MIT-licensed, take only `.obj` meshes in the assembled pose, and return a disassembly order with paths; ASAP adds gravity, stability of each sub-assembly, and a limit on how many parts are held ([Assemble-Them-All](https://github.com/yunshengtian/Assemble-Them-All), [ASAP](https://github.com/yunshengtian/ASAP), first-hand). Both need a compiled C++ simulator and pinned dependencies, and the README calls package versions "critical", which argues for a prebuilt container image. Collision is point-versus-signed-distance-field, so accuracy is tied to mesh vertex density.

Reported performance bounds what a verdict can mean. A 2026 follow-up, GLARE, reports solving 206 of 240 assemblies (**85.83%**) on the ASAP test set against 82.08% for ASAP, with the residual failures attributed to missing rotational motions, missing subassembly planning and interlocking parts ([arXiv 2609.17263](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.17263), search summary, not opened). Mean disassembly time per part in the original paper is reported at roughly 7 to 30 s (search summary, with the table extraction described as ambiguous). The inferences are that a 20-part check costs minutes and belongs behind cheaper gates, and that a failed search on a valid assembly happens roughly one time in six, so the verifier must report "no plan found within budget" with the stuck part set and never "unassemblable".

The strongest published case for putting a physical check inside a generation loop comes from a different domain. BrickGPT reports 98.8% stable brick structures with a validity check and physics-aware rollback, against 24.0% without rollback ([arXiv 2505.05469](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05469), search summary; the ablation figures come from a secondary site).

### Dynamic simulation is the wrong first tool for threads, press fits and snap fits

Contact-rich simulators can now handle nut-and-bolt and sub-millimetre insertion, but they are tuned per asset and aimed at robot policy training. One 2026 preprint reports mesh-versus-SDF contact succeeding at moderate tolerances and failing at the tightest because of mesh discretisation ([arXiv 2609.21761](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.21761), search summary, not opened). No quantitative source was found for accuracy at 0.1 mm clearance, and nothing was found on snap fits, press fits, cables or flexible parts in an automated verifier. The inference is to treat fasteners and fits symbolically: recognise the feature, replace it with an insertion direction and a clearance budget, and check the corridor geometrically. Physics earns its place for one question geometry cannot answer, which is whether a partial assembly stays put under gravity before it is fastened.

### A tiered geometric check is feasible; tool access has to be built

A targeted search found no maintained open library that checks screwdriver or wrench access to fasteners, no open tool-envelope library, and no open design-for-assembly calculator. Two projects ship related logic: cadclaw, described as an automated check suite for STEP assemblies with sequenced part removal and exploded views, and APLAN, an assembly-planning workbench that must be compiled into FreeCAD. Both are known only from search summaries and their internals are unverified.

The researchers propose a tiered verifier, ordered by cost. After static interference, build a directional blocking table: translate each part along the six principal directions plus any axes extracted from holes and fasteners, and record what it hits. Then peel greedily, removing any part with a free direction; the reversed order is an assembly sequence and parts never freed are "trapped", reported with their blockers. Then sweep a cylinder for each screw head plus a driver envelope outward along the fastener axis and intersect it with the parts present at that step. Escalate to sampling-based path planning only for parts that fail straight-line removal. This scheme is sound when it finds a sequence and incomplete when it does not, since it misses rotations, screw motions and multi-step paths, so failures should read "no straight-line removal". Parts modelled at nominal size will collide along their own insertion axis, so recognised fasteners and fits need exempting or shrinking before the sweep.

On the role of the model, published LLM and VLM assembly planners sit around 60% for plan generation and name collision and fine geometry as failure points (search summaries). The inference is that the agent should propose the base part, sequence and subassembly grouping, and the geometric verifier should accept or refute; checking a proposed order step by step is far cheaper than blind search and localises failure to one step.

## FEA in the loop works as a fixed kit with a declarative study; contact and bolts stay outside it

### Every credible loop keeps solver decks out of the model's hands

The published loops share one shape: the agent writes CAD plus a small declarative study, and a deterministic kit meshes, solves with CalculiX on quadratic tetrahedra (C3D10), and returns typed numeric findings. All paper findings in this section are search summaries. Hephaestus-CCX reports mean requirement pass rising from 19.4% to 29.3% with feedback, with per-item time growing from about 10 to nearly 70 minutes as loops lengthen ([arXiv 2605.17448](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.17448v1)). Physics-in-the-Loop reports reaching a target safety-factor band of 2 to 5 in 49 of 83 successes (59.0%) with FEA feedback; its comparison arm appears only in a truncated snippet and is unverified ([arXiv 2605.19717](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.19717)). The one system where the model generates simulation code itself reports the lowest FEA automation, 70 to 82% ([arXiv 2511.17511](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.17511v1)). Two design points follow as inferences. A two-sided target such as a safety-factor band or a mass limit alongside a stiffness limit is needed, or the loop "fixes" stress by adding material. And iteration brings instability: Physics-in-the-Loop notes that more iterations make results less stable, which argues for keep-best-so-far logic and a stop rule.

A recurring failure is the verifier being unable to bind a requirement to the artefact. Some Hephaestus strict-pass flips were "checker-contract repairs" where the checker could not find what it needed, and an independent audit of a commercial CAD agent on the same prompts found valid STEP assemblies with 38 intersecting pairs, unholed fastener interfaces and one false pass from success flags, concluding that "validity alone is not a sufficient completion criterion" ([Reify issue #37](https://github.com/QiuYi111/Reify/issues/37), first-hand). The artefact must therefore carry machine-readable part names, materials and named faces.

### text-to-cad's FEA skill is the most concrete open reference

Everything in this subsection is first-hand from the skill files. The skill runs a linear-static study on STEP with netgen meshing (not Gmsh) and a bundled CalculiX 2.23, single-threaded with a direct solver for reproducibility, and describes itself as "a first-pass engineering check, not a certification" ([fea SKILL.md](https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/blob/main/skills/fea/SKILL.md)). The agent lists faces with geometric hints, writes a strict JSON study with units encoded in key names, and receives `result.json` with findings (`severity`, `code`, `message`, `location`, `hint`), typed checks with `value`, `limit`, `ratio` and `status`, plus images and mesh files ([study-file.md](https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/blob/main/skills/fea/references/study-file.md)). Its guards are the part most worth copying: unknown keys refused, under-constrained parts refused, a solve refused when reactions do not balance applied load, automatic re-mesh at half element size when the safety factor falls below 3, and a `mesh_not_converged` flag when the peak moves more than 10%. Reference timings are about 10 s and 1.8 GB at 140,000 degrees of freedom and 74 s and 4.3 GB at 207,000 ([linear-static.md](https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/blob/main/skills/fea/references/linear-static.md)). The solver is therefore not the bottleneck in an LLM loop.

For assemblies, touching parts are bonded into one conforming mesh with per-part materials, and every part must reach a fixed part through bonded neighbours or the study is refused as `not_connected`. The stated caveats define the boundary of the open state of the art: "Bonded joints are stiffer than bolted ones and exaggerate edge stress", and "parts that press and slide on each other, and bolts, are not in this version". Vibration, buckling, heat, fatigue and impact are also excluded, and materials are assumed isotropic, so 3D-printed anisotropy is not handled. The CalculiX solver is separately pip-installable as `cad-ccx` for Linux x86_64 and aarch64 ([earthtojake/ccx](https://github.com/earthtojake/ccx/blob/main/README.md), first-hand).

### Contact and preload are scriptable but fragile; modal is the cheap next step

No published agent loop runs bolted or sliding-contact assembly FEA unattended. The pieces exist: pygccx (GPL-3.0) wraps Gmsh and CalculiX with contact, tie, coupling and pre-tension keywords, buckling and frequency steps, and bolt examples ([pygccx](https://github.com/calculix/pygccx), first-hand at the README and directory level; the bolt-helper API was not read). The traps are real. One report says a pre-tension section on a tetrahedral mesh keeps a load path through elements that touch the cut only by an edge, so "the preload is largely lost" while the solve converges ([PrePoLix PR #208](https://github.com/KwentiN-ui/PrePoLix/pull/208), search summary; the project page returned 404). No quantitative evidence on convergence rates of automated contact setup was found.

The researchers' proposed ladder is an inference. Always run geometric pre-checks. Default to bonded linear static plus modal on the same mesh. Next, tie only washer-footprint areas at bolted interfaces, extract interface forces per bolt, and run analytic bolt checks on those forces; no published agent was found doing this. Reserve full frictional contact with preload for a few critical joints under human review. A free-free modal run doubles as a connectivity test, since more than six near-zero modes means a floating part. For a gantry the highest-value additions beyond stress are static stiffness at the tool point and first natural frequencies with moving masses lumped, both linear and cheap. Predicted frequencies should be read as optimistic: one study of a printer frame found the FEA model "incapable of predicting some low-frequency high-amplitude vibrations" (search summary of [an IEEE study](https://aminer.org/pub/60b9a4dee4510cd7c8fc5ee3/mechanical-vibration-analysis-of-a-gantry-d-printer)).

Boundary conditions are the standing integration problem. Face numbers change between revisions, and text-to-cad's own docs say to "re-list faces before re-running on a new revision". The durable options are named faces authored in the CAD script or geometric re-selection each revision, in both cases verified numerically by matched area and reaction balance. No tested recipe was found for passing build123d shapes to Gmsh in-process with tags preserved; the only evidence is a CadQuery plugin's note that in-memory transfer crashes with PyPI wheels ([assembly-mesh-plugin](https://github.com/CadQuery/assembly-mesh-plugin), first-hand). That needs local testing.

On feedback, give the agent numbers and use images for location only. There is no benchmark of vision models reading stress contours; the adjacent evidence is a measuring-instrument benchmark where the best model read real gauges with 30.2% accuracy ([MeasureBench, arXiv 2510.26865](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26865), search summary). On trust, none of the surveyed agent papers was confirmed to validate its FEA kit against analytical or NAFEMS references, and no ready-made NAFEMS pass/fail suite for CalculiX was found, so the regression set of closed-form cases, unit-system canaries and must-refuse negative tests has to be built in-house. Open implementations of bolt (VDI 2230), bearing-life (ISO 281) and gear-rating standards are tiny or of doubtful maintenance, and none can be recommended as validated.

## text-to-cad covers the static verifiers; motion, assembly, tolerance and loop control are yours to build

### What earthtojake's repositories contain

The profile lists 20 public repositories, and one dominates ([repository list](https://github.com/earthtojake?tab=repositories), first-hand). `text-to-cad` is MIT-licensed under "Thompson Labs LLC", with 26.2k stars, 2.5k forks and 1,510 commits; the earlier landscape report's star figures of 9,300 and 22,000 came from secondary sources and are superseded. The older `cad-skill`, `urdf-skill`, `sendcutsend-skill` and `cad-viewer` are archived and folded in. `ccx` packages CalculiX as a wheel, `kicode` packages KiCad, and `step.parts` is a separate catalogue service of "16,000+ open source STEP parts" with a public search API, whose models carry mixed third-party licences including CC-BY-SA ([step.parts README](https://github.com/earthtojake/step.parts/blob/main/README.md), [third-party notices](https://github.com/earthtojake/step.parts/blob/main/THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md), first-hand).

text-to-cad 0.11.9 is a local engine and not a loose set of scripts: a `cad` command, a warm build server, a browser viewer and eight skills totalling about 724 KB of instructions. Under Claude Code the agent surface is the skill and the command line; the MCP component is a thin shim that "holds no tool" ([cad-mcp README](https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/blob/main/packages/cad-mcp/README.md), first-hand). A model is a decorated Python function returning a build123d shape. The worker memoises every kernel operation, so the skill's example shows 2.61 s cold and 0.01 s warm, and each face keeps a name derived from feature history. `cad model inspect` returns the source file and line that created a face, which lets a finding point at code ([inspection-and-validation.md](https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/blob/main/skills/cad/references/inspection-and-validation.md), first-hand). Ten releases shipped between 7 and 11 November 2026, and the repository's benchmarks measure engine speed only; there is no task-accuracy result.

Its loop rules are prose, and they are good ones: "A number is never read off a picture"; "A check that fails or cannot decide has shown nothing about the design"; "A retry that leaves out or shrinks a specified feature … builds a different design from the one asked for"; "Report what a check cannot see beside what it passed" ([cad SKILL.md](https://github.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/blob/main/skills/cad/SKILL.md), first-hand). No retry cap is written anywhere.

### Adoption constraints

text-to-cad requires Python 3.13 or later and pins build123d 0.13 with OCCT 8, deliberately, because it "reaches into" build123d's internals. agentcad pins build123d below 0.11, so the two cannot share an environment; build123d-mcp spans both. text-to-cad's netgen wheels are absent on Linux ARM, where a solve refuses. Telemetry is on by default and the first run downloads its runtime. The design assumes a person at a desktop with a persistent local server, and its behaviour in an ephemeral headless container was not tested. Interfaces changed across five migration guides, so pin a version. Whether maintainers accept external verifier skills upstream is unknown.

## Off-the-shelf parts: generate what is standard, proxy what is branded, and type-check every mate

### bd_warehouse is the clean tier, and its bearing data is under-used

For a build123d agent, bd_warehouse is the only library found that is native, Apache-2.0, keyed to standards, and equipped with interface helpers ([bd_warehouse pyproject](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gumyr/bd_warehouse/main/pyproject.toml), first-hand). It covers ISO, DIN and ASME fasteners, five bearing families, threads, gears, flanges, pipes, sprockets, and an OpenBuilds module with V-slot and C-beam rail, lead screw, couplers and NEMA 17 and 23 motors with named joints, which its docs call "experimental and subject to change". Fastener classes expose `types()` and `sizes()` so an agent discovers valid sizes instead of guessing. Its deep-groove bearing table already contains dynamic and static load ratings, fatigue limit, speed limits, mass and catalogue designation, even though the geometry class does not surface them ([bearing CSV](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gumyr/bd_warehouse/main/src/bd_warehouse/data/single_row_deep_groove_ball_bearing_parameters.csv), first-hand). The companion bd_materials gives representative densities but states it "is **not a materials database**".

The open parametric coverage has holes exactly where a gantry needs parts: MGN-style profile rails and carriages, ball screws, GT2 belts, pulleys and idlers, non-V-slot 2020 extrusion, and motors with real variants. That list is an inference from the documented module contents. NopSCADlib has most of these as OpenSCAD "vitamins" but is GPL-3.0 and in another language; its category list is a useful ontology of what a printer-class machine needs ([NopSCADlib readme](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nophead/NopSCADlib/master/readme.md), first-hand). No numerical audit of any of these libraries against the standards was found.

text-to-cad has no catalogue. Its step-parts skill was removed in 0.11 ("step.parts stays a service of its own"), and purchased parts enter as vendor STEP files read in the model body. Its documentation does record the artefacts a verifier must expect from vendor geometry: a motor drawn as one solid with its output fused to its case, and mating parts "drawn sunk into each other (… a carriage drawn inside its rail)" ([cad SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/earthtojake/text-to-cad/main/skills/cad/SKILL.md), first-hand). Raw vendor STEP therefore breaks naive interference checks.

### Vendor terms rule out anonymous automated fetching

Every vendor finding here is a search summary of pages that could not be opened, and none of it is legal advice; re-verify wording before relying on it. McMaster-Carr has an official product-information API, gated by a per-customer client certificate, and its terms are reported to tie CAD models to deciding "whether to purchase Products from us" and to prohibit copying or mirroring ([mcmaster.com/help/api](https://mcmaster.com/help/api)). Whether that API serves CAD files was not confirmed. MISUMI's CAD terms are reported to prohibit "acquiring Data in bulk" and providing data to third parties ([MISUMI CAD terms](https://th.misumi-ec.com/en/contents/terms/cad.html)). CADENAS terms are reported to cap downloads at 50 per day and to prohibit "any use of the data for artificial intelligence that is not included in the service description", with a contractual penalty of €20,000 per infringement plus €500 per file ([CADENAS terms](https://www.cadenas.de/terms-of-use-3d-cad-models)). TraceParts has a documented API that requires a requested key and ties each download to a user's email ([TraceParts developers](https://developers.traceparts.com/)). Distributor APIs such as Nexar and Digi-Key serve price and stock within small free quotas; sources disagree on whether Nexar's free tier counts calls or parts. Terms for igus, SKF, HIWIN, THK, 80/20, GrabCAD and the maker vendors were not verified.

The architecture that follows is an inference. Generate standard parts parametrically. For branded parts, build in-house parametric proxies from published datasheet dimensions and store the vendor SKU and URL, so that delivered assemblies do not embed vendor CAD files. Use distributor APIs for per-design price and stock. Pursue a commercial agreement only if catalogue breadth becomes necessary.

### A thin component record and a no-free-text rule

No adopted open schema bundles geometry, interfaces, ratings and SKU for mechanical parts; the nearest industrial standard is the Asset Administration Shell "Technical Data" submodel, useful mainly for property naming (search summary). The researchers propose a minimal record in which each field serves a verifier: identity and supplier SKUs; three levels of geometry (source, simplified proxy, collision); a kinematic split into bodies such as rail and carriage or rotor and stator; typed interface ports that map onto build123d joints; ratings with a source document and page for each value; datasheet mass; and verification metadata listing expected overlaps and keep-out volumes. Simplification should happen once at ingestion, since OpenCASCADE's defeaturing operator has tracker reports of hangs and unexpected topology changes (search summary).

On selection, no 2024–2026 study measuring LLM accuracy at choosing mechanical catalogue parts was found; that is an evidence gap. Adjacent work in photonics found that component selection "often picked inconsistent devices", and a circuit-design system grounds generation in a curated database and halts for human review when a part is missing (both search summaries: [arXiv 2508.14123](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.14123), [arXiv 2601.04505](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.04505v2)). The inferred rule is that the agent never emits a free-text part number: selection is a tool call returning catalogue rows, instantiation accepts only a catalogue identifier, and an out-of-catalogue request halts.

In the loop, bought parts should be treated as immutable (hash the geometry and fail on any change), mates should be type-checked port to port, ratings should be compared with loads from the motion and FEA verifiers, and every solid should resolve either to a custom part or to a catalogue entry with a supplier. Open sizing tools are thin: one deep-groove bearing-life calculator and nothing packaged for ball screws, linear guides, stepper torque curves or belts. The standard formulas for these are short, but they were not sourced in this research and should be implemented from the standards and manufacturer catalogues, with unit tests. No open dataset of stepper torque-speed curves was found; they live in per-product PDFs.

## Build order for a small team: lint assemblies first, then kinematics, then modal

Everything in this section is this report's synthesis of the researchers' inferences. It has not been validated by running any of the tools.

### Loop design rules the evidence supports

The first rule is three-valued verdicts. text-to-cad's interference, manufacturability and FEA commands and build123d-mcp's `verify_spec` independently arrived at pass, fail and not-checked, with not-checked never read as a pass. Assemblability needs the same treatment with a stated method and completeness, so the agent knows how far to trust a negative.

The second is a claim contract enforced by the harness and not by prose. The measured gain in "Done Is Not Correct" came from requiring a measurement before a completion claim. text-to-cad leaves this to skill text with no retry cap. A harness hook that refuses a "done" without a fresh verdict for every applicable verifier is the highest-leverage piece of loop control you can add, alongside an iteration cap, keep-best-so-far, and a guard that diffs the feature list against the spec so a retry cannot quietly drop a required feature.

The third is declared intent over inferred intent. Press fits, modelled threads, vendor parts drawn overlapping and sliding pairs all look like defects to a geometric check. They should be declared per pair and reported separately with their measured overlap, so a wrong-sized "intended" overlap stays visible.

The fourth is a single machine-readable artefact that drives the loop: unique part names, materials, named faces and interfaces, joints, declared fits, catalogue identifiers, and typed requirements with limits. The same artefact binds FEA boundary conditions across revisions and tells the harness which verifiers apply. agentcad's `spec.json` and PartCAD's `requirements:` are the nearest models.

The fifth is tiering by cost with gates. Validity comes before interference, because an invalid solid produced a wrong overlap number in the prototype above. Milliseconds-to-seconds checks run on every edit. Minutes-scale checks (cold all-pairs interference, pose grids, FEA, disassembly planning) run on a milestone or before a claim. Findings should carry a rule identifier, value, limit, location, the parts by name, and a hint; the direction of a penetration is what turns a finding into a fix, and no surveyed tool documents an automatic suggested fix.

### Priorities

| Order | Build | Reuse | Why here |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | One-week trial of text-to-cad, version-pinned, in your actual sandbox | The whole plugin (MIT) | Decides adopt-and-extend versus lift-patterns; headless behaviour, Python 3.13 and OCCT 8 pins, and telemetry are untested for your setup |
| 1 | Assembly linter: all-pairs interference with allow-list and cache, contact graph for floating parts, fastener-versus-hole metadata checks, mass roll-up; claim-contract hook | `cad model interference` or the box → exact-Boolean recipe; bd_warehouse hole tables | Catches the failure modes benchmarks rank highest (missing holes, misplaced and floating parts); everything downstream assumes it |
| 2 | Typed parts catalogue on bd_warehouse plus in-house proxies for rails, belts, pulleys, screws and motors | bd_warehouse, bd_materials, step.parts API for permissively licensed models | Needed before a gantry can be modelled at all; prevents invented part numbers |
| 3 | Motion verifier: multi-joint pose-grid interference, nudge-direction test, workspace check; then MuJoCo for loop closure residuals and inverse-dynamics motor margins; headless video | text-to-cad MJCF export and `mujoco check`; `mujoco-core` ≥ 4.0.1 | The largest capability nobody ships; kinematic half is cheap, dynamic half is where MuJoCo is needed |
| 4 | FEA extensions: modal step, tool-point stiffness probes, named-interface binding, regression suite of closed-form cases | text-to-cad `fea` skill or `cad-ccx` with your own kit; pygccx for frequency steps | Linear, deterministic and directly relevant to gantry stiffness; contact and preload stay human-reviewed |
| 5 | Assemblability: directional blocking table, greedy peel, fastener driver corridor | trimesh/FCL for sweeps; ASAP in a container for escalation | Cheap once interference exists; tool access is unshipped anywhere open |
| 6 | Sizing checkers and tolerance: bearing life, stepper margin, belt and screw checks fed by simulated loads; 1D stack-ups and rebuild-at-extremes | bd_warehouse bearing data; dimstack | Closes the loop from simulation to part selection; depends on 2, 3 and 4 |
| 7 | Evaluation set | bd_warehouse OpenBuilds C-beam axis as code; Voron Trident as a reference target | No public benchmark covers assemblies with motion or FEA |

For the evaluation set, the OpenBuilds C-beam axis is the only reference machine that already exists as build123d code with joints, under Apache-2.0. Voron Trident and 2.4 publish CAD under GPL-3.0, with the bill of materials in a web configurator rather than a file, so a frozen snapshot is needed; which file formats and assemblies the repositories contain was not inspected ([Voron-Trident README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/VoronDesign/Voron-Trident/main/README.md), first-hand). RatRig V-Core 3 is CC BY-NC-SA and Prusa's CORE One uses a restrictive licence of Prusa's own, so both suit internal reference only.

The adopt-versus-build decision at step 0 turns on one question that documentation cannot answer: whether text-to-cad's server model runs cleanly in your container. If it does, adding your verifiers as skills that share its stable references, poses and JSON conventions is the shortest path. If it does not, the pieces are separable: `cad-ccx` installs alone, MuJoCo is a single wheel, the interference recipe is documented in a pull request, and the conventions are free to copy.

## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the problem from "which simulator" to "which verdicts can be trusted, and who forces the agent to obtain them". The open ecosystem moved fast in 2026 and one project now covers posed interference, bonded FEA and simulation-model export from build123d; what it and everyone else leave open is anything that requires motion through time, a solved closed loop, contact, tolerance, or a person's hand and tool. Those gaps line up with what a 3D-printer gantry needs most: CoreXY belts are closed loops, dual rails are over-constrained, bolted joints set the real stiffness, and the build has to be assemblable. A team that builds the kinematic motion verifier, the modal and stiffness checks and a cheap disassembly check on top of a strict assembly linter will be working past the published frontier, with no benchmark to confirm it, which makes an in-house evaluation set part of the product and not an afterthought.

The largest uncertainties are the things to verify before committing: text-to-cad's verifiers are a week old and unmeasured; every FEA-loop and motion-paper figure here is from search summaries; the proposed CoreXY tendon model, washer-footprint bolt method and directional-blocking assemblability check are untested designs; vendor CAD terms were read only through search fragments; and no evidence exists yet on whether any of these verifiers improves agent outcomes on assemblies of more than a handful of parts.
